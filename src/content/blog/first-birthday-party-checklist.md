You meant to plan a small party. Now there are a dozen tabs open: a balloon shop, a cake site, and a friend who "kind of" wants to invite the whole class. Take a breath. A first birthday is a smaller, calmer job than it looks, and there is an order to doing it in. Here it is.

## Start with the guest list, not the cake

The question I hear most often around this party is some version of: how do I invite separate classes to a twins' birthday party? The short answer is that you don't have to. The real question is which kids your child actually knows well, and for a 1-year-old that list is usually much shorter than two classes.

### Count adults and children separately

Write the guest list in two columns: adults, and children. That's not fussiness. It's what makes the rest of the planning possible.

Say you're expecting 12 children and 18 adults, and treat that as an illustration, because the real numbers are yours. Eighteen adults is a real feeding question. Twelve children under 10 eats about half the savory food of an adult, and counts fully for cake and juice. Two numbers, two different shopping lists. If everything sits in one pile of names, you end up buying food for a room that isn't real.

### Whole class, small group, or neither

For twins, and for any first birthday, you have three honest options:

- **One joint party, both classes' regulars.** The kids from both classes who are actually friends with the twins. Bigger and more energetic, more food and more cleanup, but both twins share the spotlight.
- **Two smaller parties, one per class.** Each twin gets a day, and each party is half the headcount. More planning, and you'll be buying the cake twice, but every kid in the room knows the birthday child.
- **Friends only, no classes at all.** The smallest and calmest option: the handful of kids your child sees every day, plus the grown-ups. Save the whole-class celebration for the second birthday, when the games get better and the attention span has grown.

Whichever you pick, one thing varies a lot by place: how a school lets you invite its class. In some countries and schools the invitation goes through the class parent, in others through the teacher, and some schools don't encourage it at all. Ask before you print.

### Do parents stay?

Decide this before anything else, because it sets the headcount, the food, and the space. If parents stay, you're planning a party for thirty people. If you ask for drop-off and pickup, you're planning a party for twelve children with a few responsible adults. Both are completely fine. The point is to decide, and to say it in the invitation, so nobody improvises at the door.

## Pick a time that works around nap time

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine, a US body, recommends 11 to 14 hours of sleep in a 24-hour period for children 1 to 2, including naps. Many 1-year-olds still nap twice a day, and many drop to one afternoon nap somewhere in the second year, so follow your own child's schedule rather than any general one.

The practical rule, from my experience with these parties: start 30 to 60 minutes after a nap, and run the party for about an hour and a half to two hours. That's the window where the birthday child is rested, fed, and in it, and it's short enough to end before the next nap hits.

Here's a sample run of show for a 2:30 p.m. party after the afternoon nap, the kind you can keep on the fridge:

- **2:30 – 2:45: Arrivals.** Guests trickle in. Quiet music, a first look at the balloons, everyone settling.
- **2:45 – 3:15: Free play.** A sensory bin, bubbles, the play corner. Light snacks come out.
- **3:15 – 3:45: Food.** Everyone sits, the energy settles.
- **3:45 – 4:00: The cake.** Song, the flameless candle, the smash-cake photo. This has a hard stop: it happens at this time even if the day ran long.
- **4:00 – 4:20: A few gifts.** Open a couple, or save the rest for home.
- **4:20 – 4:30: Wind-down.** Quiet corner, goodbyes, everyone out before the next nap.

Two things make the difference. The **quiet corner** — a blanket, a book, a cushion, somewhere a child can decompress when the room gets loud. And the **hard stop on the cake**: if the birthday child is wobbling before 3:45, cut the gift opening entirely and save it for home. Cake first, then everyone heads out while the day is still a good memory.

## The first birthday party checklist: six weeks to the day

A first birthday doesn't need a long runway, but it needs an order. Here's the checklist I'd tick off, in sequence.

**Six weeks out**

- [ ] Pick the date, around a nap, not across one.
- [ ] Set the guest list policy: whole class, small group, or neither.
- [ ] Check how the school wants class invitations handled, if you're inviting any.
- [ ] Decide whether parents stay.
- [ ] Book the space, or clear the room at home.

**Four weeks out**

- [ ] Send the invitations, with an RSVP date two weeks before the party.
- [ ] Pick the one thing you want to be the best: the cake, the food, or the photo corner.
- [ ] Order the cake, or line up the baker.

**Two weeks out**

- [ ] Confirm the headcount, adults and children separately.
- [ ] Buy the decorations: a few balloons, a banner, maybe one backdrop for photos.
- [ ] Choose two or three activities. Two or three is the ceiling, not the floor.
- [ ] Note any allergies and dietary needs that came back with the RSVPs.

**One week out**

- [ ] Buy the food, or place the order.
- [ ] Ask one or two people to help on the day: food, camera, balloons-down.
- [ ] Charge the camera and the phone.

**Two days before**

- [ ] Set up the quiet corner.
- [ ] Inflate and secure the balloons, and keep the uninflated ones out of reach.
- [ ] Lay out everything the day needs, so morning-of is just execution.

**The day**

- [ ] Start 30 to 60 minutes after the nap.
- [ ] Cake at its hard-stop time, whatever the day has done.
- [ ] Collect broken balloon pieces the moment one pops.
- [ ] End before the next nap.

## What to feed (and not feed) at a first birthday

Keep the food simple, and keep it safe. The safety part is where a first birthday is different from any other party, because the youngest guests in the room may not be a year old yet.

**Honey.** The American Academy of Pediatrics, a US medical body, says not to give honey to babies under 12 months, because of infant botulism. It's fine after the first birthday, so the birthday child is safe — but a younger sibling, or a guest's baby, might not be. Keep honey out of anything those babies could taste, including the cake if it's a shared one.

**The choking list.** The CDC and AAP both name whole grapes, nuts, popcorn, and hard candy as choking hazards for young children. The fixes are easy: quarter the grapes (or cut them lengthwise), skip the popcorn, and keep the hard candy out of toddler reach. Hot dogs, if you're serving them, get cut lengthwise into quarters.

**Balloons.** The US Consumer Product Safety Commission says children under eight can choke or suffocate on uninflated or broken balloon pieces. So keep the uninflated ones out of reach, and the moment a balloon pops, collect the pieces and bin them. That's the single most valuable thing an adult can do at a toddler party, and it takes ten seconds.

**Allergies.** Ask about allergies and dietary needs on the RSVP, and write them down next to the name the moment they come back. One line in the invitation is all it takes.

**The candle.** If you want the blow-out moment, use a flameless, LED candle. It looks like a candle on camera, and there's no open flame near a room full of toddlers. It's the small swap that lets you relax at the best moment of the party.

For quantities, take the guest list apart the way you built it — adults and children separately — and use the rule of thumb that children under 10 eat about half an adult savory portion, and count them fully for cake and juice. At a pizza party, plan 2 slices per child, so one large pizza feeds 4 kids. The [food-per-person guide](/blog/how-much-food-per-person-for-a-party/) on this site has the full cheat sheet, including what to buy for adults who stay.

One last note on the sources: the honey, choking, and balloon guidance above comes from US bodies — the AAP, the CDC, and the CPSC. If you're somewhere else, check your local health authority, because the exact lists and ages can differ.

## Invitation wording you can copy

The awkward parts of a first birthday are the invitations, so here's wording you can copy and make your own.

**The twins-and-two-classes note.** At this age, "the class" is the daycare or nursery group, so if the twins aren't in one yet, just invite family and the regulars. Keep it plain, and give people an easy out.

> We're celebrating the twins' first birthday on Saturday, June 12 at 2:30 p.m., at our home. We'd love the friends from both classes, but if you'd rather just send the ones they see every day, that's completely fine. Please let us know by May 29 how many little ones are coming.

**The "parents stay" line.** Say it up front, with the practical detail.

> Parents are welcome to stay the whole time — there's coffee, and the kids can come and go. If you'd rather drop them off and pick them up later, just say so when you reply.

**The "no gifts" line.** Normal, warm, and unambiguous.

> You don't need to bring a gift — we'd just love to see you. If you'd like to bring something, a children's book for the twins would be lovely.

**The sibling reply.** When a parent asks to bring the baby sibling, a warm no that keeps the door open.

> We're keeping the number of kids small this year so the day stays calm, so we're only inviting the regulars for now. The twins would love to play with their sibling at the next one.

## Keep the budget small without it feeling small

A first birthday doesn't have to cost much, and there's no "correct" number to spend. The move that works is picking the one thing you want to be the best — the cake, the food, or a photo corner — and letting the rest stay simple.

Say the whole party is 500 in your currency, and treat that as just an example, because the real number is whatever's comfortable for you. If the cake is the splurge, most of the money goes there, the decorations stay a few balloons and a banner, and the favors either don't happen or get made at home. If the food is the splurge, you do the opposite. The point isn't to spend less for the sake of it. It's that once you've decided what matters, the small choices stop being decisions.

Three quiet money-savers, from parties I've actually hosted: home-made snacks beat a catered table at this size. Borrowed decorations from a friend cost nothing and rarely get noticed as borrowed. And a single photo corner, with one backdrop and a phone propped up, beats a photographer for the whole afternoon.
