You already know who you want in the room. The hard part is everyone else: the cousin from a funeral three years ago, your mom's book club, the coworker who will hear about it on Monday. Taking a name off feels like a verdict, so the list sits there until the caterer asks for a number.

That guilt is normal. How to cut wedding guest list politely is not a gift for letting people down. It is a cap, a letter next to each name, and a few short messages you can copy. The rules do the uncomfortable work.

## How to cut wedding guest list politely

Start with a number, not a debate about who loves you.

Pick the smaller of two limits. One is the seats the room can hold, including the two of you. The other is the seats the food budget can feed. If the venue fits 110 and the menu covers 75, the list is 75. If the menu covers 120 and the room fits 80, the list is 80. Write that number at the top of a page. Everything after this is fitting names under it.

Paper is enough. A spreadsheet is enough. Three columns: name, whose side, and a letter. You need a ceiling both of you will say out loud before either of you calls a parent.

Three things that are not a ceiling:

- "We'll see how the replies come in." Plan for the yes.
- "We'll invite them and hope they decline." Some of them will come, and you will be paying. A hope is not a plan.
- "Mom can have whoever she wants." That is how 70 becomes 110 by Thursday.

If you cannot agree on the number, you are not ready to cut names. Count heads, not invitations. A couple is two. "Daniela and guest" is two people, and the second one has no face yet. If you would not pay for a stranger, the invitation needs a name.

## Sort every name into A, B, or C

Twenty quiet minutes, in different rooms. Each of you writes every possible name and marks one letter.

**A — the day is worse without them.** Parents, siblings, the friend you would call from the parking lot. They stay, even if the menu gets simpler.

**B — you would be glad to see them, and the day still works if they are home.** They get a seat only after the A's are in and the number still has room.

**C — you are inviting them so nobody gets upset.** You cannot picture them at a table. The only sentence you have is "we have to." These are the cuts.

Then merge.

- A name on either person's A list stays. You do not veto your partner's closest friend.
- A name on both C lists goes. If you both feel only guilt, the guilt is not a relationship.
- Everything else is B, decided by the number, not by who argues louder.

If the A's already overflow the room, a few of them have to become B's in the same sitting. Do not let one A list eat the other person's B's.

Number the B list. Number 1 is the first seat that opens if someone declines. The last number is the first cut. When a parent asks about a cousin, you can say "they were 14, and we had seats through 9." That is kinder than "we don't really know them."

| Letter | What it means | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | You would notice the empty chair | Invite |
| B | Happy if they come | Invite while seats remain, in the order you numbered |
| C | Guilt, or fear they'll hear about it | Do not invite |

Keep the letter and the plus-one on that same page. A C slides back on when the list lives in three text threads.

## Rules you can point to when someone pushes back

Rules feel cold. They are also how both families get the same answer. Write them under the cap before you tell anyone.

**You have not spoken in two years.** No real conversation in two years — a call, a meal, more than a birthday comment — and that person is a C, unless one of you would be heartbroken. A childhood nickname, or "they came to my brother's wedding," is not a current relationship.

**Plus-ones have a definition.** Married, engaged, or living together. Write the partner's name down. Everyone else is invited by name. A single friend gets "and guest" only if they would otherwise know nobody in the room. A nameless plus-one is a stranger at the table, and a headcount that grew without a decision.

**Work is almost none.** Invite the people you see outside the office, and tell them it is because you are friends. Do not invite half a department. If you cannot defend every name to the rest of the team, invite none of the loose colleagues. A card and cake the week after covers everyone else.

**Kids are a policy.** All kids, or no kids. One written exception, then stop: children in the wedding, or babies who are not walking yet. "Our nieces, but not your kids" is the feud.

**Both families get the same kind of cap.** If one side has 40 A names and the other has 22, you do not hand the larger side 18 extra seats to keep the peace. Show both lists. Parents may have a block — "10 seats each, for people we would not have thought of" — and when the 10 are used, the block is closed.

**Do not invite a hoped-for no.** If you cannot afford a yes, do not send the card. Parents can suggest names. They do not get an open pile of invitations, even if they are helping to pay, unless that pile has a price. Show the cost of the extra seats, then decide if the money is for those people or for the band.

## Scripts you can copy

Short is better than a long apology. A long one sounds like the decision is still open.

### What to say to parents

Say this on a call or in person. A text is easy to forward at midnight.

> The room and the budget cap us at 80 people, for both families together. We want your siblings and their partners. We can't add the cousins we haven't seen in years. The same rule is on the other side, and our own guilt-names are already off. Can we sit down Sunday and fit your names into the seats we have? You have 10 seats to use however you want.

If they say it is unfair:

> I know it feels unfair. Both sides have the same limit. I hate disappointing you. The cap stays.

If they offer to pay for more people, price it before you answer, then cap it in the same conversation.

> If you cover 8 more seats, we can add 8 names you choose from the B list. After that, we're done.

### A friend who will not get a plus-one

Send this before the invitation, so the card is not the surprise.

> We would love you at the wedding. Plus-ones are only for people who are married, engaged, or living together. I know that's a disappointment, and I'm sorry. You will know a lot of people there. I really hope you come.

If they push:

> I wish I could say yes. We used the same rule for everyone, including our own single friends. I can't make one exception without reopening the list.

Do not invent a venue rule. If the room is not the reason, the rule is the reason. A fake excuse turns into a feud when they hear the real version later.

### No-kids wording

Use the same sentence on the invitation or in a note to parents. Do not soften it differently for each family.

> We love your children. The wedding will be adults only, and we hope you can get a sitter and celebrate with us. Babies who aren't walking yet are welcome on a parent's lap.

If a flower girl is invited and their child is not:

> The only children invited are the ones in the wedding. We kept that exception small so we weren't choosing among everyone else's kids. I'm sorry. I know that's a hard one.

### Work, and a yes you have to take back

> We're keeping the wedding to family and close friends, so I won't be handing out invitations at the office. I'll bring cake the week after.

To the one teammate who is actually a friend:

> You're invited because we're friends, not because we share a team. I'm not inviting the rest of the office, and I wanted you to hear that from me.

If you already promised a seat and the cap will not hold it, say so before anyone books a flight.

> I need to take back an invitation, and I'm sorry. We promised a seat before we had a real number, and we can't offer you one anymore. I hope I can take you to dinner after the wedding.

Then stop. Offer the dinner only if you mean it. Do not promise a seat "if someone cancels" unless you will actually call.

## What each cut guest saves

Use your own quotes. If you do not have them yet, this is a planning rule of thumb for a dinner wedding, not a national average. Build the line from food, drinks, a chair and a favor, then tax or service of about 18 to 25 percent.

One worked example:

| Line | Per guest |
| --- | --- |
| Plated dinner | $95 |
| Beer and wine | $35 |
| Cake, favor, rental chair | $15 |
| Tax and service at 20% on the $130 of food and drink | $26 |
| What leaves with that guest | $171 |

One name is about $170 here. Until you have a quote, use **$150 to $200 per cut dinner guest**.

- **Ten cuts** in this example are about **$1,700**.
- **Twenty cuts** are about **$3,400**.
- **Twelve unnamed plus-ones** are about **$2,050**.

The photographer, the dress, and most DJs cost the same at 60 guests and at 100. Cutting 10 people shrinks the lines priced per head. It does not shrink the whole wedding by 10 percent.

Read the minimum. If the contract requires 80 guests, cutting from 100 to 80 saves 20 times the per-person price. Cutting from 80 to 70 saves nothing until you renegotiate. A half-price kids' meal, often around $45, still uses a seat a B-list friend might have had.

Write $171 next to the cap. Adding a name is that decision. Keep food and drink as their own budget line, on paper or in a sheet, and change it when the headcount changes.

## Say it once, then stop reopening the list

The feud usually starts on the third conversation. The first is a disappointment. The third is a negotiation, because a no was treated as a draft.

- **Tell both sets of parents the same week,** with the same number, after your own C names are already off.
- **Bring the ranked B list and a block of seats.** They are spending a fixed number, not building the wedding.
- **Suggested names stay off the invitations** until you review them once. "The rule is the same on both sides" is the only comparison you need.
- **"I hear you. I'm sorry it hurts. The answer is still no."** "We have 80 seats" is the whole budget speech.

If a parent may actually stop speaking to you, move them to the A list and cut somewhere else. Break a rule once, out loud, not in secret for whoever shouts. Then send invitations only from the final list, and give the caterer that count.

## Keep the cuts from sliding back

A parent will remember a godparent. A friend will assume a plus-one. Someone will reply yes for a person you never named. The fix is the page you already made: the letter, the cap, the dollar figure.

Look at it when you order invitations, when replies are due, and when you send the final headcount. Silence at the deadline is a no for the caterer. A spreadsheet works if you open it. A page on the fridge works if you cross names out.
