## Two pieces of paper, two different jobs

I still remember the evening my first batch of cards went into envelopes. The date was booked, the venue was signed, and I was staring at two stacks of stationery — save the dates and formal wedding invitations — with no idea which one I was holding. The save the date vs wedding invitation question is one of the first that trips up first-time hosts: which goes first, how far out does each one go, and why does everything on the internet seem to disagree by a month or two?

There isn't one rule, there's a rhythm — and the rhythm exists to protect one thing: the RSVP deadline, which is the number everything else hangs off. Work backwards from it and the save the date and the invitation fall into place on their own.

A quick note on the guidance in this post: it's US etiquette as commonly given by the planning sites couples actually use. Customs and what counts as "polite" differ by country, so if you're planning somewhere else, treat the ranges as a starting point and adjust. Here's the job split:

| | Save the date | Formal invitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Job** | Gets the date onto the calendar while it's still possible to keep | Asks the question and gets a yes or no |
| **Send** | 6 to 8 months before (8 to 12 for destination or holiday weekend) | 6 to 8 weeks before (10 to 12 for destination) |
| **Tone** | Casual is fine — email or text counts | The formal one, even for a casual wedding |
| **What it carries** | Names, date, city, "a formal invitation will follow" | Date, time, venue, reception details, RSVP line with a deadline |
| **Who gets one** | Every household you'll invite, and no one else | Every household you'll invite, and no one else |

That last row matters more than people expect, and it's the answer to a question I get asked more than any other: *if I'm sending save the dates, do I send them to people I might not invite?* No. A save the date is a promise of an invitation. If a household gets a save the date, they get an invitation. No exceptions, or you've just taught your guest list that your stationery is a preview.

## When to send save the dates: the 6-to-8-month window

Send save the dates **six to eight months before the wedding**. That's the window every major US planning source lands on, and it exists for a practical reason: that's when guests book travel, save money, and ask their employers for the days off. Send earlier and the date is still so far away it fades; send later and the guests who need a flight or a hotel block are already squeezed.

Nudge the window when the circumstances push:

- **Destination or far-flung wedding:** eight to twelve months ahead. Passports, flights and time off all take longer.
- **Holiday weekend:** lean to the eight-month end of the window. Long weekends fill with other travel and other weddings first.
- **Peak season (spring and fall):** lean earlier within the window.
- **Mostly local guest list:** sit closer to the six-month mark and keep leeway in case the date moves.

**Too early:** more than a year out is generally too early — plans change, and a date that far away drifts off people's radars.

**Too late:** under four months out, save the dates stop making sense. Just send the invitations early and let them do the whole job.

What the card itself should carry is short — four things, nothing more:

> Save the date — Sunday, 16 May 2027
> Emma & Jonas
> Copenhagen · Denmark
> A formal invitation will follow.
> More details: emmaandjonas.example

If your wedding is casual, this can be an email or a text. A save the date is the one piece of wedding stationery where the format doesn't have to match the formality of the day.

## When to send the wedding invitations: 6 to 8 weeks

The formal invitation goes out **six to eight weeks before the wedding** — ten to twelve if guests are traveling. Both edges of the window matter:

- **Earlier than eight weeks** and the card gets filed in a drawer and forgotten.
- **Later than six weeks** and the RSVP deadline arrives before guests have had time to check calendars, arrange childcare, or ask for leave. That's when you spend a month chasing answers you should have been given calmly.

And here's the part that anchors the whole timeline: **set the RSVP deadline three to four weeks before the wedding** (about six weeks for a destination wedding). Work backwards from your caterer, who needs the final headcount roughly a week before the day — seven to fourteen days, depending on who you're working with. Set the deadline a few days to a week before that cutoff and you land on three to four weeks out. Those buffer days are where you chase the last non-responders and finish the seating.

The invitation itself should say everything a guest needs to say yes, and nothing that belongs on a save the date:

> Emma & Jonas
> request the pleasure of your company
> on Sunday, 16 May 2027
> at 3 o'clock in the afternoon
> Church of St. Ansgar, Vesterbrogade, Copenhagen
> Reception to follow at Frederiksborg Castle
>
> Kindly reply by **Friday, 16 April 2027**

That last line — a **specific date**, not "as soon as possible" — is the single most useful thing on the card. Guests who see a real deadline reply earlier, and you get a hard line to work from when the non-responders show up (which they will — the follow-up has its own post: [RSVP No-Shows: What to Do When Guests Don't Reply](/blog/rsvp-no-shows/)).

## When the timing goes wrong: a recovery plan

The rhythm above assumes the venue is locked by the time the save the dates go out. It isn't always. When the plan slips:

- **You're past the save the date window but still months out.** Send a digital save the date or a short text this week. Format doesn't matter to guests; silence does.
- **The wedding is under four months away.** Skip save the dates entirely. Mail the invitations at the eight-week mark and don't apologize for it — guests don't audit your stationery schedule.
- **You've sent save the dates and the date changed.** This is the one where honesty beats elegance. Send a short correction to every household, name the change plainly, and don't bury it in small print. A template that works:

  > Hi [name] — a quick update: Emma and Jonas's wedding has moved from 16 May to 29 May 2027. The venue and city are unchanged. The formal invitation (or an updated one) will follow by [date]. No need to reply — just hold the new date. We're sorry for the shuffle.

- **The venue changed but the date didn't.** Same move, same honesty, same channel — and if the new venue needs longer travel lead time, push the invitation date up to the eight-week mark.
- **You're behind on everything.** A digital save the date now, invitations at eight weeks, and an RSVP deadline of three weeks if you can't get four. A compressed timeline beats a perfect one that's late.

The pattern behind all of these: correct fast, correct honestly, and never let a guest discover a change from a friend first.

## The second wave: people you didn't know about

There's a question that comes up a lot in planning threads: *would you even accept a wedding invitation if you only found out because someone else cancelled?* The host-side version is what you'll actually face: people you should have invited, discovered *after* the first wave went out. The cousin whose new partner you'd never met. The work friend who only just told you they're coming to town.

A late second-wave invitation carries real risk: a guest who learns second-hand can feel like a second choice. You genuinely didn't know, so don't overthink it. What you can control is how the invitation arrives:

- **Call or message, don't just mail the card.** A card that lands weeks after their friends received theirs says less than you'd like. Thirty seconds on the phone says a lot.
- **Be warm and specific.** "We'd really love to have you" beats "you're invited."
- **Don't over-explain.** One honest line about how you found out is enough.
- **Keep the RSVP math honest.** If there's room for the standard 3-to-4-week reply window, keep it; otherwise ask for a nearer reply date and tell the caterer the extra count is provisional.

And the hardest version of this: you invite someone *because* another guest declined or cancelled their spot. Send that invitation within a few days of the decline, not the next time you sit down with the stationery. Keep the tone warm and personal, and don't mention who declined or why — the guest doesn't need that context to be glad they're invited. Reactions vary by person, and there's no rule that predicts any of it; a short note that says you'd love to have them beats a formal card that arrives late with an apology attached. If the decline is the start of a bigger conversation about who stays on the list, that's a different post ([how to cut a wedding guest list without a feud](/blog/how-to-cut-wedding-guest-list/)).

A note that works for the second wave:

> Hi [name] — I'm sending the wedding invitations out, and I realized I'd been meaning to invite you since [one honest line: "before you moved to another city"]. It genuinely would mean a lot to have you there. The wedding is [date] in [city] — details attached. Please let me know by [date] if you can make it.

For the decline case (don't imply they're a second choice, don't mention who declined):

> Hi [name] — we'd love to have you at our wedding on [date] in [city]. Details attached; could you let me know by [date]?

This is the part I lose track of most easily, because it's a handful of personal conversations instead of one batch of 60 envelopes. I give every second-wave conversation a due date on my to-do list, and I keep a "sent" column and a "note" column next to each household — the same two columns work in a spreadsheet, and a good guest-list tool gives you the same thing with reminders that actually fire.

## A note on the October send

Mailing save the dates in October (USA) puts them just ahead of the holiday mail rush. A few points, all US-specific but the problem is universal wherever you have a busy postal season:

- **The holiday card flood.** The weeks before Christmas carry the year's heaviest mail volume, and USPS publishes holiday mailing deadlines each year. Your save the date doesn't need to arrive by Christmas, but it does need to arrive while people are still reading mail.
- **Check your local cutoffs.** Every post office has its own practical deadline for holiday-bound mail. If your mailing window lands in the busy season, check usps.com or ask your post office rather than assume.
- **Don't let your date get lost.** Mailing a few weeks early, or using a digital send, keeps your card out of the flood.

## A worked example

Put it together and a mid-August wedding (local, US timing) looks like this:

| When | What |
|---|---|
| **December 2026** (8 months out) | Save the dates out to every household on the guest list |
| **20 June 2027** (8 weeks out) | Formal invitations mailed, RSVP deadline printed on them |
| **18 July 2027** (4 weeks before) | RSVP deadline — start following up with non-responders |
| **8 August 2027** (1 week before) | Final headcount to the caterer; seating chart done |

Every row works backwards from the caterer's number. If your dates are different, start at the bottom and walk up.
