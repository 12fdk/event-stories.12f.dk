You've done the venue, the menu, the guest list. Then comes the quiet question that keeps people up at night: how many gifts do I actually put on the registry? Too many and you look like you're asking for a lot. Too few and your guests wander around a half-empty list, end up buying the same $40 candle four times, or give up and pick something they have no idea if you want.

Here's the relief: **a big registry is not greedy, and a short one is the real problem.** A thin list forces guests to buy off-list, creates duplicates, and leaves latecomers with nothing they can afford. So the goal is to make it easy for every guest to find something they can afford and genuinely want you to have.

## How many gifts: the formula that actually works

Forget counting heads. **Count households.** A couple, a family, or a group of friends almost always gives *one* gift together, so a 100-person wedding usually means 50–65 invited households, not 100.

A common rule of thumb to start from: **multiply your invited households by 1.5 to 2.** Run 50–65 households through it and you land around **75–130 items**.

That gives you a target range, not a magic number. And nobody buys everything on the list, so the extra items cost you nothing.

| Invited households | Items to aim for (× 1.5–2) |
| --- | --- |
| Under 30 (small wedding) | 45–60 |
| 30–50 | 45–100 |
| 50–65 (~100 guests) | 75–130 |
| 65–95 (~150 guests) | 100–190 |
| 95+ (150+ guests) | 145+ |

Build toward the **upper** end of the range. A depleted, under-stocked list is what forces duplicates and pushes guests off-list — and an over-stocked one is harmless, because not every item will be bought. You can always trim; you can't un-sell a sold-out list.

## The price points matter more than the count

The total number is only half the answer. What guests actually struggle with is *finding something in their budget.* The under-$50 tier is the one that matters most, because that's what coworkers, acquaintances, and shower guests buy.

A healthy price mix for a ~100-item list looks like this — an illustrative split in US dollars, 2026; local prices differ. Treat the shares as guideline proportions, not measured data:

| Price tier | Share of the list | Who buys here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under $50 | ~30–35% | Coworkers, acquaintances, shower guests |
| $50–$100 | ~30–35% | Friends, extended family |
| $100–$250 | ~20–25% | Close friends, siblings |
| $250+ | ~10% | Close family, group gifts |

If you have fewer than a couple dozen items under $50, you'll lose most of your lower-budget guests. A registry that's all high-priced items looks like it's only for the people who can afford to give a lot — which it absolutely isn't.

## The cash question, done without the awkwardness

For decades, "we'd prefer money" was the wedding faux pas. That's changed. Asking for cash is now completely acceptable when it's done the right way — and the right way comes down to **where and how you ask.**

**Do this:**
- Put the cash fund on your registry or your **wedding website**, not the invitation.
- Name what it's *for* — "our first home," "the honeymoon," "the trip to Portugal" — so it reads as a gift, not a bill.
- Pair it with physical gifts so traditional guests have a comfortable option.
- Keep the language warm and optional: *"if you'd like to contribute, our honeymoon fund is at [link]."*

**Don't do this:**
- **Never** print the cash fund or registry details on the invitation card itself. That's the one rule that hasn't changed, and it's the fastest way to feel like you're asking for money.
- Don't say "in lieu of gifts" or "no boxed gifts" — it reads as presumptuous and takes options away from guests.
- Don't pressure anyone. A single gracious sentence on the website is enough; repeating it across five group chats starts to feel like a running request.

The safest setup: a cash fund as *one option* on the website, with 5–10 physical items alongside it. That covers everyone.

## How to keep the list as clean as it is complete

A registry isn't done the day you build it — it moves. Items sell through, a shower guest buys something early, and you want latecomers to still find a good option. That's why keeping the list in one shareable place, updated as things change, beats a paper list that goes stale.

A simple rule of thumb for staying on top of it: **check the list after each major milestone** — after your engagement party, after the showers, and a few weeks before the wedding. Add anything that sold through, and make sure the under-$50 tier is still stocked for the guests who haven't shopped yet.

A good wish-list tool makes this painless: it keeps your list in one place, turns it into a clean PDF you can share with guests, and lets you refresh and re-export it as your plan changes — so nobody's left staring at a half-empty page.

## The guest-side questions: handmade, "do I have to," and cash

A lot of the rest of this lands on the *guest* — the people your registry has to serve. A few keep coming up.

### Is a handmade gift a bad gift?

Couples (and guests) ask whether a handmade blanket, a quilt, or a knitted throw is odd to give. The short answer: **handmade is usually welcome when it's personal and useful.** A hand-stitched blanket for the nursery or a quilt made by a grandmother carries meaning that a store-bought gift can't. Keep it low-pressure on the receiving end: pair it with a card, keep it a size the couple can actually use, and be gracious if they end up with several of the same thing — you can't ask a relative to unpick their knitting.

### Do I have to buy a gift at all?

A gift is customary but it's never *owed*. The invitation is a gesture, not an invoice. If you can't afford to give something, a thoughtful card says as much as a box does. Nobody should spend into debt over a gift.

### What if I can't attend — does my gift change?

**No — it doesn't need to match what a guest who attends gives.** Expectations are lower when you can't be there. A common US figure for 2026 puts a declined-invitation gift in the **$30–$75 range** — or a heartfelt card with a smaller cash gift — and amounts vary by country, culture, and how close you are. For a very distant connection, a sincere card on its own is fine.

And this varies by culture and country: in parts of East Asia and South Asia, cash in an envelope is the norm, and amounts follow local custom rather than a US dollar range. If a guest simply asks "can we just give cash?", the gracious reply is yes — name a purpose on your registry or website and let them contribute, and keep a few physical items alongside so traditionalists still have an option.

## The timing and thank-you notes

Gifts arrive on a schedule, and the schedule is looser than people think:

- **Before the wedding** — the ideal window is roughly two weeks out, so the couple receives it without the chaos of the day itself.
- **At the wedding** — bring a card or cash in a sealed envelope to the card box; avoid hauling wrapped boxes to the venue.
- **After the wedding** — Emily Post and most etiquette guides now put the window at **about three months**; the old one-year rule has largely fallen out of use. If you're past it, send it anyway with an apology note — late beats never.

And the thank-you note matters as much as the gift — especially for a cash contribution. Name what the money went toward. "Your contribution to the honeymoon fund" lands as personal in a way a generic "thank you for the gift" doesn't.

## A gift for the bride — and when fewer gifts arrive

### The post-wedding gift

You'll see this one too: a friend or sister who was part of the wedding now wants to give the bride *something* a few weeks later, once the honeymoon is over. There's no rule that requires a post-wedding gift — it's entirely optional — but it's a sweet one when it happens. Good options that need no big budget: a short personal note ("thank you for the best day I've been part of" lands more than anything wrapped), a shared meal with the new couple a month in, or help — running the thank-you cards, the photos, or the return shipping of gifts the couple got too many of. The post-wedding moment is when couples feel the most seen.

### If fewer gifts arrive than you expected

It's the quiet, unglamorous question that shows up after every wedding: "we only got so many gifts, and we spent a lot on the day." The honest answer is the one nobody wants to hear but everyone needs: **the gifts are not a refund of the wedding bill.** Guests give what they can and what fits their relationship to you. Many give cash, many give late (up to three months after), and many give small. A modest registry on a modest budget is not a failure. If the list looked thin, it usually just means the list was thinner than it needed to be — which is what the 1.5–2× rule is for. The thank-you note still applies to every gift, no matter the amount: name the gift, thank the guest, and let them know you used it.

You don't need a perfect registry. You need enough items, spread across the price points your guests can actually afford, with a cash option for the people who'd rather give money — all in one place that stays updated as things sell through. Count households, multiply by 1.5–2, load up the under-$50 tier, put the cash fund on the website not the invite, and refresh the list after each milestone. Do that and your guests won't just give you a gift — they'll find the one you actually wanted.
