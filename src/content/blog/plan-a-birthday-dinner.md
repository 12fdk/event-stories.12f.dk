You have a date, a rough list of names, and a question that is easier to avoid than to answer: if I'm throwing a birthday dinner for myself, who pays? It comes up in the group chat before the menu, before the venue, before anything else is settled. And the host-burden version is the same question in reverse: why does everything about this evening land on the person with the least free time that day?

Take a breath. A birthday dinner is a small event. The whole thing is one table, one meal, and a few decisions made once, in the right order. Here is the order that works: settle who pays, send the invitations and collect real numbers, set a budget per person, pick a menu you can finish cooking early, build the evening hour by hour, and protect the day itself.

## If you cook and provide the room, you pay

The default for a hosted dinner is that the host covers food and drink, so if you're doing the cooking, the bill is yours. A home-cooked dinner often runs about $12–25 per person for all food and drink (US figures, 2026; local prices differ), and picked-up catering trays are often $40–75 per person before tax and tip. That is the range to plan against.

The split that fits a birthday you're hosting for yourself: you cover the room, the main, and the drinks, and each guest brings a side, a bottle, or a dish. It only works if the invitation says so. One line does the job: "I'm doing the cooking and the wine. Please bring a side or a bottle you like."

Three models actually work:

- **You pay, full stop.** Fine for a small guest list and a menu you can make ahead. It's the least friction for your guests and the least awkward.
- **The split.** You provide the room, the main, and the drinks. Each guest brings a side, a bottle, or a dish. It works well for a birthday dinner with a larger list, and it moves a real share of the food bill to the guests if people follow through.
- **Per-person contribution.** You pick a catered menu or a nice restaurant and ask for a flat amount per guest in advance. Works when you're not cooking at all, and it ends the "who's chipping in" conversation, because the chip-in happened in week one.

Whichever you pick, **say it in the invitation**. That one line settles the who-pays question and turns a vague social expectation into something people can actually do.

## Send invitations and collect RSVPs

The number that drives the budget, the portions, and the seating is the count of people who confirmed, and that number only exists once the invitations have been answered. A few moves that keep the count real:

![An open black planning notebook with a pen and a stack of blank cream cards on a round wooden table, soft natural light](/blog/plan-a-birthday-dinner-img1.webp)

- Send the invitations four to six weeks out for a home dinner. Less than two weeks out is a guess in disguise.
- Ask for a yes or no, and give a date by which you need it. "Let me know if you can make it" collects maybes, and maybes are what you over-buy for.
- Chase the quiet ones once, with a line you can copy: "No pressure at all, but I'm finalizing the menu this week and I'd love to know if you're in." One chase is normal; a second one is where it stops being.
- Keep the dietary notes where the list lives, so the menu is built on allergies and preferences rather than on memory.

## Budget per person, then multiply by confirmed guests

Here are the numbers a home party often runs (US figures, 2026; local prices differ):

- **Home-cooked, all-in:** often $12–25 per person for everything edible and drinkable.
- **Catering trays:** often $40–75 per person before tax and tip.
- **A restaurant, with a set menu:** whatever the menu says, plus tip.

Worked example: 20 confirmed guests, home-cooked, at $18 per person is a $360 food and drink budget. Twenty guests, catering trays at $45 is $900 before tax and tip. Two different evenings at the same table. That gap is the menu choice.

A few moves that move the number the right way:

- **Buy the expensive thing for everyone and the cheap thing in volume.** A good main and a plain side reads as generous at half the price of two good mains.
- **Drinks are the line item easiest to keep in check.** Wine at one bottle per two adults, one non-alcoholic per guest, and a pound of ice per guest keeps drinks from becoming the biggest line of the bill.
- **Multiply by confirmed guests, not invited guests.** An RSVP you never chased is a number you're still paying for in your head.
- **Hold 10–15% back.** The extra guests, the extra ice, the "can you pick up a bottle of something" at the store on the way.

## Pick the menu and the portions

The menu should be chosen so that cooking day is boring. Boring is the goal: 80% of the work done the day before, the oven doing the last 20%.

Portions for a real seated dinner, from the [food-per-person guide](/blog/how-much-food-per-person-for-a-party/) on this site:

| Part | Per person |
|---|---|
| Raw boneless protein (seated dinner) | about 8 ounces (6 cooked) |
| Cooked starch, as a side | about 1/2 cup |
| Dry pasta (with appetizers) | 3 ounces |
| Dry pasta (pasta is the meal) | 4 ounces |
| Grazing hour, bites | 10 per person |
| Wine | one bottle per two adults |
| Ice | a pound per guest |

Two rules keep this from overshooting:

- **If a grazing hour precedes the meal, cut the bites in half.** People eat the crostini, then sit down hungry for the thing you spent the afternoon on. Ten bites becomes five.
- **Kids under 10 eat about half an adult savory portion.** Count them fully for cake and juice.

And if guests are bringing food, a dish brought for the meal goes on the table. If someone hands you a food gift, it is fine to keep it for later unless they plainly brought it to share.

## If it's a secret, a co-planner settles the who-pays model

When the birthday person can't be involved, a co-planner owns the model: they pick it, they say it in the invitation, and they answer the questions. Keep the need-to-know circle to two or three people. ## Build the evening hour by hour

The plan that keeps the cook sitting down is an hour-by-hour run of show, and a dinner needs less of one than a wedding:

![A kitchen counter at golden hour with a ceramic pan of roasted vegetables, a bottle of red wine and two glasses, and a candle-lit set table visible through the doorway](/blog/plan-a-birthday-dinner-img2.webp)

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 17:00–17:50 | Buffer. Sides and prep are done; the table gets its final pass before anyone arrives. |
| 18:00 | First guests arrive. Drinks out, grazing on the table. The main is in the oven. |
| 18:30 | Door person answers, pours, and keeps the kitchen going. |
| 19:00 | The main comes out of the oven. Plates are ready to go. |
| 19:30 | The main hits the table. This time is fixed, and the rest of the evening bends around it. |
| 20:00 | The toast, and the cake if it's a cake night. |
| 20:30 | Coffee, dessert, and 30 quiet minutes for the cook. |

The two non-negotiables are the buffer before the first arrival and the 30 quiet minutes for the cook. Everything else is negotiable. The [dinner party timeline](/blog/dinner-party-timeline/) walks through building this kind of run of show for the whole evening.

## Protect the day itself, in one checklist

- Shop the morning of: ice (a pound per guest), the fresh bits, and the drinks you didn't buy ahead.
- Set the table before the first arrival. An empty room that becomes a set table is a different party from a set table that gets rushed.
- Name the door person before the first arrival.
- If a menu step needs help, that's the step you cut or hand off.
- Take the photo, put the phone away, and sit down at your own dinner.
